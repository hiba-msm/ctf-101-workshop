Part 1 · The hook

What is cybersecurity?

Protecting accounts, devices and data from being stolen, damaged or misused, and understanding how attacks work so we can stop them.

Student accounts

Your email, learning platform and Wi-Fi logins, protected by passwords and 2FA (a second proof, like a code on your phone).

University websites

The grades portal must show your grades, and only yours.

Personal data

Names, student ID numbers, addresses, payment details. Valuable to criminals, protected by law.

Spotting phishing

Phishing = "fishing" for your password with a fake message used as bait.

Understanding how apps get exploited

An exploit uses a mistake (a "bug") in software to make it do something it was never meant to do. Defenders study exploits so they can find and fix bugs before criminals do.

It's much more than "hacking"

Cybersecurity people…

defend systems
investigate incidents
build secure software
break things ethically to fix them
write policy and teach others

~2 min. Keep it concrete. Ask: "What would happen if someone got into your university email?" (password resets for every other account, impersonating you to professors…)

Optional extra: professionals often summarise the goals as the CIA triad: Confidentiality (only the right people can see it), Integrity (nobody can secretly change it), Availability (it works when you need it).

Part 2 · Understanding CTFs

So, what is a CTF?

Capture The Flag is a treasure hunt, except the clues are hidden in files, websites, network traffic, code and encrypted messages.

ChallengeA puzzle with a hidden secret inside.
FlagThe secret text that proves you solved it.
CategoryThe type of puzzle: crypto, web, forensics…
PointsHarder challenge = more points. Most points wins.
SubmitPaste the flag into the competition website to score.

Anatomy of a flag

FLAG{you_found_the_secret}
FLAG = the prefix{ } = the wrapperinside = the secret text

Every competition picks its own format, e.g. picoCTF{…} or HTB{…}. The rules page always tells you.

Real challenges include dead ends too, and that's part of the fun.

~2 min. Analogy: a treasure hunt / escape room for computers. The clue trail can go through several "stations" before you reach the flag.

Flags are plain text that you copy and paste. They are designed to be unguessable, so finding one proves you solved the challenge.

Part 2 · Understanding CTFs

How does a CTF work?

From signing up to climbing the scoreboard, in eight steps.

01 · JOIN

Register yourself or a team on the CTF website.

02 · BROWSE

See the board of challenges by category and points.

03 · CHOOSE

Pick one that matches your interest or level.

04 · INVESTIGATE

Download the file or open the website it gives you.

05 · SPOT CLUES

Anything odd? The title, file names, formats, hints.

06 · TOOLS + LOGIC

Test ideas one at a time with the right tool.

07 · FIND IT

Spot the text in the agreed flag format.

08 · SUBMIT

Paste it in, earn points, climb the scoreboard.

Typical event: online, 24–48 hours, solo or in teams. Practice platforms have no timer at all, so you can learn at your own pace.
Stuck? Many beginner platforms offer hints. After a competition, players publish writeups: step-by-step explanations of their solutions.

~2 min. Walk through the line left to right. Stress step 5: most of the work is noticing details, not typing commands.

Writeups are the secret weapon of every learner: reading how others solved a challenge you couldn't solve is how most people improve.

Part 2 · Understanding CTFs

Types of hackers

They use similar skills, but differ in permission, intent, and goal.

White hat

Authorized security professionals who protect systems.

  • Permission from the owner
  • Ethical hacking and audits
  • Risk reports and responsible disclosure

Red team

Authorized attackers who test whether defences work.

  • Penetration testing
  • Simulates real-world attacks
  • Finds weaknesses before real attackers do

Blue team

Defenders who monitor, detect and respond to attacks.

  • Security monitoring
  • Incident response
  • Patch, contain and recover
Gray hat: May find weaknesses without clear authorization. It can still break laws or rules.
Black hat: Breaks into systems for theft, disruption or personal gain. This is illegal and harmful.
Purple team: Red and blue teams share findings to improve defences together.

The same technical skill can be used safely or dangerously. Ethical security work requires permission, clear rules and responsible reporting.

~1.5 min. Explain that “hat” describes intent and authorization, while “team” describes a role in a security exercise.

Red team focuses on testing through attacks; blue team focuses on defence and response. Purple team is collaboration between both.

Stress that gray-hat activity is not automatically safe or legal, and CTF practice should stay on deliberately authorized platforms.

OSINT

Difficulty

Open Source Intelligence: answering questions using information that is already publicly available. ("Open source" here means public, not free software.)

Campus SnapshotOSINT · 100 pts
"Our club posted this photo from last year's event. In which city was it taken? Flag format: FLAG{city}"
event_photo.jpg
FLAG{...}Submit

Illustrative challenge, written for this workshop

Clues to look for

Signs and shop namesLanguage on signsLandmarks and skyline Car number platesShadows and weatherUsernamesHidden photo metadata
Metadata = "data about data". A photo file can secretly store the camera model, the date and sometimes GPS coordinates. The standard format for photo metadata is called EXIF.

~1.5 min. Ask the room: "Looking at this picture, what would you search for first?" Get 2–3 answers. Usually: the sign, the tower, the language.

Important nuance: most social networks strip EXIF metadata when you upload a photo, but CTF files often keep it on purpose.

Ethics: OSINT challenges use material made for the challenge. Never use these techniques to track real private people.

02
Category · Beginner → Advanced

Cryptography

Difficulty

Secret messages, and figuring them out. First, learn to tell three very different things apart:

Encoding

Changes the format. No secret key: anyone can reverse it. Like writing a sentence in Morse code.

Hi → Base64 → SGk=

Encryption

Scrambles with a key. Only someone with the key can unscramble it. Like a locked box.

HELLO → Caesar, key 3 → KHOOR

Hashing

A one-way fingerprint. You can't turn it back, but the same input always gives the same output.

MD5("password") =
5f4dcc3b5aa765d61d8327deb882cf99

Clues: how to recognise what you're looking at

Ends with = or ==letters, digits, + and / only → probably Base64
0–9 and a–f only→ hexadecimal (two characters = one byte)
01000110 01001100…groups of 8 bits → binary, one character each
SYNT{...}word-shaped, letters shifted → Caesar / ROT13
exactly 32 hex characters→ could be an MD5 hash
Shifty BusinessCrypto · 50 pts
"Julius left a note for the club: SYNT{ebg13_vf_rnfl}"
Hint in the title: Julius… Caesar?

Illustrative challenge, written for this workshop

~2 min. The #1 beginner confusion: calling Base64 "encryption". It isn't: there's no key, anyone can decode it.

Hex: computers store everything as bytes (8 bits, i.e. eight 0/1 values). Hexadecimal is a shorthand that writes each byte as two characters from 00 to FF.

MD5 is obsolete for security, but it shows up in CTFs. "Cracking" a hash means guessing inputs (often from a wordlist of common passwords) until one produces the same hash; you never actually reverse it.

The SYNT clue: FLAG shifted by 13 letters becomes SYNT. Spotting your flag format in disguise is a classic trick.

02 · Cryptography

Crack a Caesar cipher live

InteractiveFull guide
SYNT{ebg13_vf_rnfl}

Drag the slider until the text makes sense. Trying every possible key like this is called a brute-force attack, and with only 25 keys it takes seconds.

Bonus: XOR, the CTF favourite

XOR compares bits: different = 1, same = 0. Applying the same key twice gives back the original.

01000001 'A' ⊕ 00100000 key = 01100001 'a'

A beginner's game plan

  1. Encoding, cipher or hash? Look at the characters and the length.
  2. Try the simplest ideas first: Base64, hex, ROT13.
  3. Still stuck? Ask an identifier tool to guess the cipher.
  4. Peel layer by layer: challenges often stack several encodings.

Tools: why and when

CyberChefbrowserThe "cyber Swiss army knife". Chain operations like recipe steps; its Magic operation auto-detects common encodings.
dCodebrowserUnknown classic cipher? Hundreds of solvers plus a Cipher Identifier.
PythonoptionalRepetitive or custom? A few lines of code beat 1000 clicks. Learn it later.
Practise CryptoHack CyLab library (Cryptography)

~2 min. Live demo: drag the slider slowly from 0 up to 13 and let the room shout when it becomes readable: FLAG{rot13_is_easy}. ROT13 is a Caesar shift of 13, and applying it twice gives back the original (26 letters).

You can type any text into the box, e.g. a volunteer's name, and show what a shift of 3 does.

Higher difficulty crypto (RSA, AES misuse) needs maths, which is what CryptoHack teaches step by step, for free.

Steganography

Difficulty

Hiding a message so that nobody even notices it exists. From the Greek for "covered writing".

Cryptography

Everyone can see there is a secret, but they can't read it. 🔒

Steganography

Nobody even notices there is a secret at all. 🫥

Innocent CatStego · 150 pts
"Just a cute cat picture… or is it?"
cat.png · 2.8 MB

Illustrative challenge, written for this workshop

Clues to look for

File much bigger than expectedOdd metadata commentsNoise in one areaReadable text inside the fileAudio with strange noise

Normal view: the hidden bits change each colour by at most 1 out of 256, which the eye cannot see.

Each pixel's colour is stored as numbers like 10110101. Changing the last bit (the "least significant bit", LSB) hides one bit of a message per pixel.

~1.5 min. Click the button to reveal the hidden word. The pixel values really differ only in the last bit, just like real LSB steganography.

Key distinction: crypto hides the meaning, stego hides the existence. In CTFs they are often combined: the hidden text might also be Base64.

03 · Steganography

How to approach it

Full guide

A beginner's game plan

  1. Read the metadata (ExifTool).
  2. Look for readable text and check the file size.
  3. Browse colour channels and bit planes.
  4. Check for files appended or hidden inside.
  5. Password hint in the description? Try steghide with it.

Tiny example: data after the end

A PNG image officially ends with an IEND marker. Image viewers ignore anything after it, but the bytes are still there:

Digital Forensics

Difficulty

Being a detective for computers: examining digital evidence to reconstruct what happened.

Broken AssignmentForensics · 150 pts
"This was submitted as essay.pdf, but nothing can open it. What is it really?"
essay.pdf

Illustrative challenge, written for this workshop

Metadata

Who made a file, when, with what software.

File carving

Recovering files from raw data, even deleted ones, by searching for signatures.

PCAP

A "packet capture": a recording of network traffic, like CCTV for a network.

Logs

Diaries kept by systems: who logged in, when, from where.

File signatures ("magic bytes")

The first bytes of a file reveal its real type, whatever its name or extension says.

FF D8 FFJPEG image
89 50 4E 47PNG image (‰PNG)
25 50 44 46PDF document (%PDF)
50 4B 03 04ZIP archive (PK…), also .docx, .xlsx, .apk

Source: List of file signatures

Binary = base 2 (only 0 and 1). Hexadecimal = base 16 (0–9, then A–F). One byte = 8 bits = two hex digits, from 00 to FF.

~2 min. Connect to slide 2: the mysterious "notes.pdf" that won't open. Check its signature!

Fun fact for the room: .docx, .xlsx, .pptx and Android .apk files are all ZIP archives inside. Rename one to .zip and you can open it.

"PK" comes from Phil Katz, the creator of the ZIP format.

04 · Digital Forensics

How to approach it

Full guide

A beginner's game plan

  1. Ask "what is this file really?"
  2. Pull out readable text.
  3. Look at the raw bytes.
  4. Open it with the specialised tool: Wireshark for PCAPs, Autopsy for disk images.
  5. Build a timeline of what happened.

Tiny example: three commands

$ file essay.pdf essay.pdf: Zip archive data, at least v2.0 $ xxd -l 8 essay.pdf 00000000: 504b 0304 1400 0000 PK...... $ strings capture.pcap | grep FLAG FLAG{packets_tell_stories}

file names the real type · xxd shows bytes in hex · strings prints readable text · | passes the output to the next command

A terminal is a text window where you type commands instead of clicking. These three are built into most Linux systems and macOS. On Windows, use WSL (Linux inside Windows).

Tools: why and when

AutopsyinstallGot a disk image? A point-and-click tool, built on The Sleuth Kit, that recovers deleted files.
CyberChefbrowserFound weird data? Decode what you extracted.
Volatility 3advancedGot a RAM dump? Lists the programs and connections that were in memory.
Practise CyLab library (Forensics) TryHackMe: Wireshark: The Basics CyberDefenders (blue-team labs)

~2 min. Walk through the three commands. The PCAP line is a typical first attempt: sometimes flags travel through the network in plain text.

Autopsy is a free, open-source digital-forensics platform that provides a graphical interface for The Sleuth Kit. Volatility analyses memory dumps (RAM snapshots), which is advanced but common in later CTFs.

Web Exploitation

Difficulty

Websites are built from many parts. One small mistake can expose data or allow something unintended.

How the web talks: HTTP

HTTP is the language of browsers and servers: a request asks, a response answers. A cookie is a small note the site stores in your browser so it remembers you are logged in (your session).

Authentication

"Who are you?" Showing your student card at the library entrance.

Authorization

"What may you do?" Your card opens the library, not the professors' office.

Student PortalWeb · 100 pts
"Our new grades portal is totally secure. Can you log in as admin?"
http://portal.ctf.example

Illustrative challenge, written for this workshop

Clues to look for

Comments in the HTMLHidden form fieldsCookies like role=studentNumbers in URLs: ?id=1042Chatty error messages/robots.txt

~2 min. Connect to slide 2: changing a number in the address bar and seeing someone else's timetable is an authorization bug (often called IDOR: Insecure Direct Object Reference). The site checked who you are, but not whether that record is yours.

/robots.txt is a public file that tells search engines which pages to skip, and sometimes accidentally lists "secret" pages.

Reverse Engineering

Difficulty

Working out how a program works, without its original source code.

Analogy: a puzzle box with no instructions. You shake it, look through the cracks and press buttons until you understand what's inside.

Source code = instructions written by humans (e.g. in C). Compiling turns it into machine code, an executable (.exe on Windows, ELF on Linux). Assembly is a human-readable spelling of machine code: tiny steps like "move", "compare", "jump". You don't need to read it on day one.
GatekeeperReversing · 200 pts
"This program asks for a password. Find it, and it will reward you."
gatekeeper (Linux executable)

Illustrative challenge, written for this workshop

Clues to look for

Readable text insideComparisons with fixed valuesFunction names like check_passwordFile type: .exe, ELF, .apk, .pyc

~2 min. Reverse engineering is also a real job: malware analysts reverse viruses to understand and stop them.

Keep assembly as a concept only. Modern tools (decompilers) translate machine code back into C-like code, so beginners rarely read raw assembly at first.

06 · Reverse Engineering

How to approach it

Full guide

Static analysis

Study it without running it: read its text, decompile it. Like reading the puzzle box's blueprint.

Dynamic analysis

Run it and observe, ideally inside a virtual machine (a safe "computer inside your computer").

A beginner's game plan

  1. Identify the file type.
  2. Run strings: sometimes the answer is just sitting there.
  3. Run it (safely) and watch what it does.
  4. Open it in Ghidra and find the password check.
  5. Rebuild the logic and get the flag.

Tiny example

$ strings gatekeeper Enter password: Wrong password! s3cr3t_k3y Correct! Here is your flag:
// Ghidra's decompiler turns machine code back into C-like code if (strcmp(input, "s3cr3t_k3y") == 0) { puts("Correct! Here is your flag:"); print_flag(); }

Binary Exploitation

Difficulty

Making a program misbehave by giving it input it wasn't built to handle.

Memory (RAM) = a program's workspace: a long row of numbered boxes, each holding one byte.
Buffer = a fixed number of boxes set aside for some data, e.g. 8 boxes for a name.
Stack = short-term memory for the function currently running, piled up like plates. Heap = memory requested while the program runs, for data whose size isn't known in advance.
Buffer overflow = writing more data than the buffer holds, so it spills into the neighbouring boxes.

Try it: overflow the buffer

buffer: name[8]
0x4011b600
saved return address

The return address is a note telling the program where to continue when a function ends. Overwrite it with garbage and the program crashes. In CTFs, players carefully control what gets overwritten.

~2 min. Type a long name live (e.g. "AAAAAAAAAAAA") and watch the yellow boxes turn red. That is the core idea of a buffer overflow, the most classic memory bug.

Real programs have defences (we'll see checksec next). Keep it conceptual: no exploit code in this workshop.

Source for further reading: CTF101: Buffer overflow.

How to approach it

Full guide

Why C and memory matter

The C language gives programmers direct control of memory, with no automatic safety checks. Copying too much data is an easy mistake to make.

Learning basic C and how memory is laid out is what makes this category "click".

A beginner's game plan

  1. Run the program: what does it ask for?
  2. Check which defences it has with checksec.
  3. Read the source if given, or decompile it in Ghidra.
  4. Find where input is copied without a size check.
  5. Watch memory step by step in a debugger.
checksec lists defences such as stack canaries (tripwire values that detect overflows), NX (memory that can't run code) and PIE (randomised addresses).

Beginner mistakes (we all made them)

Randomly trying tools without understanding the problem

First ask: what is this, and what am I looking for?

Ignoring the challenge description

Titles and puns are deliberate hints. "Julius" → Caesar.

Assuming everything is encrypted

Most beginner "secrets" are just encoded: Base64, hex, ROT13.

Giving up after the first failed attempt

Expect 5–10 wrong ideas. That's not failing, that's how solving works.

Forgetting file extensions and metadata

Run file and ExifTool first: 30 seconds, huge payoff.

Not taking notes

Write down every attempt. Tomorrow-you will thank you.

Part 5 · Let's try something! · Challenge 2 of 2

Spot the clue

A forensics challenge gives you this file. The description says: "My holiday photo got corrupted. Can you recover the memories?"

Preview not available
holiday_photo.jpg
Type (from extension)JPEG image
Size4.7 MB
Opens in photo viewer?No: "file format not supported"

What would you inspect first?

Vote first (hands up for each option), then click to see what each one would tell you.

File name: a hint, not proof

"holiday_photo" is what the creator wants you to believe. Worth noting (holiday? memories?), but it proves nothing about what the file is.

Extension: just a label

".jpg" is part of the name. Rename cat.jpg to cat.mp3 and not a single byte changes. A "JPEG" that no viewer can open is suspicious.

Metadata: good instinct!

ExifTool reports File Type: ZIP, not JPEG, and there's no camera data at all. ExifTool checks the file's real signature, so this already tells you something is off.

Contents: the smoking gun 🎯

The first bytes are 50 4B 03 04 ("PK"), the signature of a ZIP archive, not a JPEG (FF D8 FF). Rename it to .zip, open it, and inside is flag.txt.

Expert answer: there's no single right answer. Pros check all four in under a minute, but "what is this file really?" comes first, because every other clue depends on it. Quick triage: file → ExifTool → strings → hex editor.

~3 min. Take a show of hands for each of the four options before clicking anything. Then click each option in turn and discuss.

Everything shown is accurate: ZIP files start with 50 4B 03 04 ("PK"), JPEGs with FF D8 FF, and ExifTool identifies the file type from its content rather than its extension.
